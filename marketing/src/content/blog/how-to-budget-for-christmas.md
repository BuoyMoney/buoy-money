Most holiday overspending isn't one big purchase. It's forty small ones spread over six
weeks: a gift card for a coworker, extra groceries for a crowd, wrapping paper twice
because you couldn't find the first roll, a last-minute flight home. None of them feels
like a problem on the day. Together they become a credit card statement in January that
takes until March to pay off.

The fix is old and boring. Decide the number early, split it into a few buckets, and put
money aside in small amounts before December arrives. Starting in October gives you three
paychecks' worth of runway instead of one.

## Step 1: Write down last year's real number

Before you set a budget, find out what last December cost. Open your bank and card
statements for November and December and add up everything that was holiday-shaped:
gifts, food and hosting, travel, decorations, cards and postage, and the charity drives
that cluster at the end of the year.

Most people guess low here. The guess covers gifts. The real number also covers the
ham, the gas to drive to your in-laws, and the matching pajamas.

That total is your starting point. You can decide to spend less this year, but now
you're deciding against a real figure instead of a hopeful one.

## Step 2: Split it into named buckets

One giant "Christmas" line hides where the money goes. Four or five named categories make
the tradeoffs visible:

- Gifts
- Food and hosting
- Travel
- Decorations and extras (cards, wrapping, the tree)
- Giving

Names matter because they let you move money on purpose. If travel comes in under what you
planned, you can choose to shift the difference to gifts. Without the split, the overrun in
one area just quietly eats the others.

## Step 3: Divide by the days you have left

This is where starting early pays off. Say last year came to $900 and you want to hold it
to $750 this year. From October 1 to December 20 is 81 days.

$750 ÷ 81 days is about **$9.25 a day**.

Nine dollars a day is a small enough number to fit around everything else. The
same $750 started on December 1 would need $37.50 a day, four times as much, which is
why so many people end up putting it on a card.

## Step 4: Set it up so it builds on its own

You can do all of this in a spreadsheet. If you use Buoy, two features do the
accumulating for you.

First, a savings goal with a date. Create a goal called "Christmas" with a target amount and a
target date. The goal page shows what you've saved, what's left, and how much a day you'd
need to reach it in time. Add money as you go, or give the goal a monthly allocation so it
gets a share of what's left of your budget at the end of each month.

Second, count-up categories for the buckets. In Buoy, a count-up category earns a slice of its
amount every day instead of handing you the whole month on day one. Rollover is on by
default for count-up categories, so whatever you don't spend carries into the next month.

Here's how that plays out for a Gifts category set to $100 a month, starting October 1
(example numbers):

| Date | Earned so far | Spent | Available |
|---|---|---|---|
| October 31 | $100 | $0 | $100 |
| November 30 | $200 | $45 (early sale) | $155 |
| December 15 | about $248 | $45 | about $203 |

You watch the number grow through the fall, and when you do buy something early, you can
see exactly what it costs the rest of the season.

If you'd rather keep holiday money out of sight until December, set a category's end of
period action to Transfer to Savings and point it at your Christmas goal. Whatever is left
at the end of each month moves into the goal instead of staying in the category.

## Step 5: Shop against the bucket

When December comes, the question for every purchase becomes "is this in the Gifts
bucket?" instead of "can I afford this?" The first question has an answer. The second one,
asked in a crowded store in mid-December, almost always gets a yes.

A few habits help:

- Make the gift list before Black Friday, with a dollar amount next to each name. Sales
  are only savings if the item was already on the list.
- Buy the boring extras (wrapping, tape, batteries, cards) in November, when nobody is in a
  hurry.
- Leave a small buffer in Food and hosting. Someone always brings a plus-one.

## What this gets you in January

Some things will still run over in December, and that's fine. What changes is that the
money was already set aside when you spent it, so January starts with a normal bank
balance instead of a balance transfer offer.
