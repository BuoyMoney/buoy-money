The 50/30/20 rule is the budget most people have heard of and few people keep. It fits on a
sticky note: half your take-home pay goes to needs, 30% to wants, 20% to savings and paying
down debt. It was popularized by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi in their 2005 book
*All Your Worth*, and it has stuck around because it is simple enough to remember.

Simple to remember is not the same as simple to live with. Here is the math, the places it
breaks, and how I would set it up so it holds past the first month.

## Step 1: Start from take-home pay

The rule works on what lands in your account, after taxes and anything your employer takes
out before you see it. Not your salary.

Say you take home $4,000 a month (an example; use your own number). The rule gives you:

| Bucket | Share | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Needs | 50% | $2,000 |
| Wants | 30% | $1,200 |
| Savings and extra debt payments | 20% | $800 |

If your pay changes from month to month, use a slow month as the base. Budgeting from your
best month is how a plan falls apart by the 20th.

## Step 2: Sort your spending honestly

The hard part of 50/30/20 is not the arithmetic. It is deciding what counts as a need.

- Needs are the bills that keep life running and that you would still pay in a bad month.
  Rent or mortgage, utilities, basic groceries, insurance, getting to work, and the minimum
  payment on every debt.
- Wants are everything you could cut for a month without real harm. Eating out, streaming,
  hobbies, the nicer version of a need.
- Savings and debt is the money that makes next year better. An emergency fund, retirement,
  and anything you pay on a debt above the minimum.

A quick test: if you would cancel it the week after losing your job, it is a want. The
grocery bill is a need. The part of the grocery bill that is takeout on a tired Thursday is
a want.

## Step 3: Check whether 50% is even possible

For a lot of people it isn't. Rent alone can take 40% of take-home pay in many cities, and
the rule was never meant to be a test you fail.

If your needs come to 60%, change the split. Run 60/20/20 and protect the
20% for savings, or run 60/25/15 for a few months while you work on the biggest need. What
matters is that you chose the split on purpose and wrote it down. A split you picked and
keep beats a perfect one you abandon.

The opposite happens too. If your needs come in under 50%, don't let the gap drift into
wants. Send it to savings.

## Step 4: Turn the wants into a daily number

The 30% is where 50/30/20 usually fails. Needs are mostly fixed bills that take care of
themselves. Savings can go out automatically on payday. Wants are dozens of small decisions,
and a monthly number of $1,200 tells you nothing at the register on the 9th.

So divide it. Put $300 of that $1,200 toward eating out and, in a 30-day month, you have
$10 a day. Skip a day and tomorrow you have $20. A small daily figure is one you can
actually check against what is in your hand. I wrote more about why in
[why a daily allowance beats a monthly budget](/blog/daily-allowance-budgeting-explained),
and the [daily allowance calculator](/calculator) will do the division for your own numbers.

## Step 5: Pay the 20% first

Don't wait to see what is left at the end of the month. There is rarely anything left at
the end of the month. Set up an automatic transfer at your bank for payday, so the savings
leave before the wants have a chance to find them. This is the
[pay yourself first](/pay-yourself-first) idea, and it pairs well with 50/30/20.

## How to set up 50/30/20 in Buoy

Buoy doesn't have a 50/30/20 button, and you don't need one. The rule maps onto a normal
Buoy budget like this:

1. Add your take-home pay as an income category, using the slow-month number you picked earlier.
2. Add your needs as regular categories (rent, utilities, groceries, insurance, minimum
   debt payments) adding up to your needs share.
3. Add your wants as categories and switch on **Count-Up** for each one. The
   category earns its share every day instead of handing you the whole month on day one.
   That is the daily number from earlier, worked out for you.
4. Look at the line at the top of the budget. It compares your income with what you have
   given to categories. With needs and wants set, it should read about **$800 ready to
   assign**: your 20%, still waiting for a job.
5. Give the 20% a job with a savings goal. Create a goal (an emergency fund is a good
   first one) and set its **Monthly Allocation** to 100%. At the end of each period, the
   income you didn't give to a category, that $800, is recorded to the goal. Have two
   goals? Split the percentage between them.
6. Let underspending count as saving. For each count-up want, you can choose
   **Transfer to Savings** for the end of the period, so whatever you didn't spend goes to a
   goal instead of quietly rolling into next month's eating out.

Your bank connection categorizes most transactions automatically, so the tracking side
needs no typing. Buoy records these amounts but never moves money between your bank
accounts, so the payday transfer from "Pay the 20% first" still lives at your bank.

## Check it once a month, not every day

The daily number handles the wants. Once a month, look at the totals: what share actually
went to needs, wants and savings? If wants keep coming in at 35%, you have two honest
choices. Lower the count-up amounts a little, or accept 35% and take it out of somewhere
you mean to. Either is fine, as long as the number on paper matches the one you live.

The rule is a starting point. Its real job is to make you decide, ahead of time, what your
money is for, which is the same thing every budget is trying to do. If you want to go
further and give every single dollar a job, read about
[zero-based budgeting](/zero-based-budgeting). If what you want is a budget you'll still be
checking in six months, try [count-up budgeting](/count-up-budgeting).
