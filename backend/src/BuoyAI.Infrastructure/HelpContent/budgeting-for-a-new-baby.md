Somewhere around month five of a pregnancy, a spreadsheet gets made. It has a tab for the
nursery, a tab for the hospital bill, a column of estimates copied from a forum. It is a
good spreadsheet. It is also, almost always, the last time anyone opens it.

Nobody lacked discipline. A plan built for a calm evening met a household that had not
slept in a week. The budget has to work on the
worst day, not the planning day, or it does not work.

So this is the version I would build now, knowing what the first year is like.

## Separate the one-time costs from the monthly ones

Two kinds of money leave when a baby arrives, and they need different handling.

The one-time costs are the ones the spreadsheet is good at: crib, car seat, stroller, the
deductible on the birth itself. They are large, they happen once, and the right tool is a
savings goal with a target and a date. Set it, let a share of each paycheck flow into it,
and stop thinking about it.

The monthly costs are the ones that quietly eat a budget: diapers, formula or feeding
supplies, wipes, the pharmacy runs, the extra laundry, and later childcare. These are small,
frequent, and they never stop, which is the exact shape a daily allowance is built for.

## Give the baby a category, or three

In Buoy each spending category earns a daily allowance and the balance counts up when you
spend less than it. For a new baby I would create three: baby essentials (diapers, wipes,
feeding), baby health (copays, prescriptions, the pharmacy), and childcare if you have it.

Set each to a monthly figure that feels roughly right. It will be wrong. That is fine,
because the point of the first two months is to find out what the real number is, and a
count-up category tells you that without a spreadsheet: if the balance keeps climbing,
the allowance is high; if it keeps going negative, it is low. Adjust once, in month three,
when you can think again.

Every purchase syncs from your bank and lands in the right category, either by a rule you
wrote once ("Target on a Sunday morning is baby essentials") or by the AI when the rule
does not fit. Nobody is typing receipts at 2 a.m.

## Both parents, one budget, no arguing about the app

The math rarely breaks a baby budget. What breaks it is one person setting it up while
the other spends against it without seeing it.

Buoy's family sharing puts both of you on the same budget with roles. One person can own
it and change the allowances; the other can be a budget admin with the same powers, or
read-only if they just want to see where things stand before a shopping trip. Each person
keeps their own login and their own bank connections; the shared budget is the thing you
look at together.

In practice this means the question "did we already buy wipes this week?" has an answer
on either phone, which is a smaller thing than it sounds until you have had the argument.

## The numbers, and why I will not give you a total

You will find articles that put a figure on the first year. They are guessing, and the
range is so wide that the number is useless: a family with insurance that covers the
birth and a grandparent for childcare is living in a different budget from a family
paying a deductible and a daycare waitlist deposit.

What I can tell you is the shape. The one-time costs front-load into the last two months
before the due date. The monthly essentials settle into a rhythm within about eight weeks.
Childcare, when it starts, is usually the largest single line in the budget, larger than
everything else baby-related combined, and it deserves its own category from the first
invoice.

## Set it up before the baby comes

Do it in the calm months: three categories, one savings goal, both parents on the budget.
Then leave it alone. The app will keep the numbers current from your bank while you are
busy with the far more important thing.
